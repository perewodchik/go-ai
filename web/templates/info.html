Model Architecture, Mathematics & Diagnostic Guide

Understand how your Go AI learns from first principles. This guide details the mathematical formulas behind each step of the AlphaZero-style self-play loop, what happens during neural network updates, and how to diagnose issues when training stalls or behaves unexpectedly.

🚀

Quick Start Settings

For fast feedback on a standard laptop: select 9×9 board, Chinese rules, Small network (4 blocks × 64 filters), komi 6.5, MCTS Simulations 50–100, and Self-Play Games 5–10 per iteration. This setup demonstrates visible tactical learning within 1–2 hours.

Board Size

Recommended: 9×9

The $N \times N$ grid the AI learns on. Game complexity grows exponentially with board area ($N^2$). Smaller boards have vastly fewer possible positions ($\approx 10^{38}$ for 9×9 vs $\approx 10^{170}$ for 19×19), allowing the model to reach high strength in hours instead of weeks.

Bigger (13–19) Full Go strategy with complex opening frameworks (Fuseki) — but self-play games get much longer and require a larger network with significantly more MCTS simulations.
Smaller (7–9) Rapid training loop with tactical battles. 7×7 is ideal for instant verification; 9×9 is the sweet spot for watching full Go concepts emerge quickly.
Options 7 · 9 · 13 · 15 · 19  ·  ⚠ Immutable after model creation.

Komi

Recommended: 6.5

Bonus points awarded to White to offset Black's first-move initiative. The fractional $.5$ prevents ties (jigo). Proper komi ensures both players have an equal expected value from the initial position ($v(s_0) \approx 0$).

Too high (> 7.5 on 9×9) White becomes inherently favored; value head learns a positive bias toward White regardless of play quality.
Too low (< 5.5 on 9×9) Black wins almost every game; the network gets skewed feedback on opening moves.
Typical: 6.5 (9×9) or 7.5 (19×19)  ·  Keep the .5 fractional value.

Ruleset

Recommended: Chinese

Scoring algorithm for game completion. Chinese rules (Area Scoring) count alive stones plus enclosed territory ($S = \text{Stones} + \text{Territory}$). Japanese rules (Territory Scoring) count territory plus captured stones.

Chinese Clean, mathematically deterministic scoring without requiring manual dead-stone agreement. Perfect for autonomous self-play.
Japanese Traditional counting style, but requires complex dead-stone resolution phases.
Note: Self-play training target always computes rewards using Chinese Area Scoring internally.

Self-Play Games per iteration

Recommended: 5–15

The number of fresh games generated by MCTS self-play prior to network gradient updates. All positions are appended to a rolling Replay Buffer of up to 50,000 states.

More (15–50) Prevents overfitting to recent moves, providing smooth, stable gradient steps — at the cost of longer iteration times.
Fewer (1–5) Fast iteration turnarounds, but higher variance in value targets and risk of policy oscillation.
Range 1–100  ·  Default: 5

Eval Games vs random bot

Recommended: 4–10

Benchmark games played against a uniform random bot after each training iteration to evaluate win rate $W$ and update Elo.

More (10–20) Reduces variance in win-rate metrics and gives tight Elo confidence intervals.
Fewer (2–4) Saves time; slight statistical noise in benchmark metrics.
Range 1–100  ·  Default: 4

MCTS Simulations per move

Recommended: 50–200

Number of search tree expansion cycles ($M$) performed for every single move decision. This is the single most effective control parameter for move quality vs search time.

Higher (100–400) MCTS creates sharp, highly accurate search target distributions $\boldsymbol{\pi}$, accelerating neural network learning per iteration.
Lower (< 30) Search tree is too shallow; move visit counts remain near-uniform, causing policy loss stagnation.
Range 10–1000  ·  Default: 50  ·  Laptop sweet spot: 50–150

c_puct exploration constant

Recommended: 1.5

Controls the balance between exploring unvisited candidate moves (prior $P(s,a)$) vs exploiting high-value lines ($Q(s,a)$) in the PUCT search formula.

Higher (2.0–3.0) Encourages wider exploration of alternative branches; helpful early in training to prevent premature convergence.
Lower (0.5–1.0) Focuses search compute strictly on the network's top-ranked candidate move.
Normal range: 1.0–2.0  ·  Default: 1.5

Learning Rate

Recommended: 0.002

The gradient step magnitude $\eta$ for the Adam optimizer. Managed automatically via a Cosine Annealing learning rate schedule.

Too high (> 0.005) Gradient divergence, policy loss spikes, or NaN values in loss calculations.
Too low (< 0.0003) Excessively slow parameter updates; network struggles to overcome initial randomized state.
Typical: 0.001–0.003  ·  Default: 0.002

Temperature ($\tau$) & Smooth Decay Schedule

Recommended: Init 0.8 → Final 0.1 (30 moves)

Temperature controls move randomness during self-play according to $\pi_a \propto N(s,a)^{1/\tau}$. Higher temperature ($\tau \approx 0.8–1.0$) promotes opening exploration, while lower temperature ($\tau \approx 0.1$) focuses search on peak visit actions in the mid/late-game.

$$\text{Smooth Decay: } \tau(t) = \tau_{\text{init}} + \min\left(1.0, \frac{t}{T_{\text{thresh}}}\right) \cdot (\tau_{\text{final}} - \tau_{\text{init}})$$

Why Smooth Decay Matters (Avoiding Early Collapses & Pass Locks):
If temperature drops off a cliff (a hard step threshold like dropping to $0.001$ at move 15), MCTS move selection becomes 100% rigid mid-fight. If the network hasn't yet learned tactical captures or if MOVE_PASS receives even 1 or 2 visits during search, both bots rigidly pick PASS in back-to-back turns — prematurely ending the game at move 50–60 with 1-liberty groups still uncaptured!

Board Size Scaling for Temp Threshold ($T_{\text{thresh}}$):
The threshold should scale to ~50% of average game length:

  • 9×9 Board (Avg ~50 moves total): $T_{\text{thresh}} = 30 \text{ moves}$. By move 35–40, the board is in endgame mode where precision is needed to close boundaries without blundering.
  • 13×13 Board (Avg ~110 moves total): $T_{\text{thresh}} = 50–60 \text{ moves}$.
  • 19×19 Board (Avg ~200 moves total): $T_{\text{thresh}} = 80–100 \text{ moves}$.

Configurable per model in Advanced Settings  ·  Defaults: Init 0.8, Final 0.1, Threshold 30 moves (9×9)

🔬 Under the Hood: Detailed Mathematical Workflow

The AlphaZero architecture consists of a dual-head Residual Neural Network $f_\theta(s) = (\boldsymbol{p}, v)$ guided by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). Below is the exact step-by-step mathematical formulation implemented in the system.

Complete Iteration Lifecycle

Step 1
State Encoding
Board position $s_t$ converted into a multi-plane binary spatial tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times N \times N}$.
Step 2
Dual NN Forward Pass
ResNet computes prior move probabilities $\boldsymbol{p}_\theta(a|s)$ and position outcome prediction $v_\theta(s) \in [-1, 1]$.
Step 3
MCTS PUCT Search
Runs $M$ simulations using UCB selection, Dirichlet root noise, leaf evaluation, and value backpropagation.
Step 4
Action & Policy Target
Action $a_t$ sampled from temperature-scaled visit distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$. State triple $(s_t, \boldsymbol{\pi}_t, z_t)$ sent to buffer.
Step 5
Loss & Gradient Step
Network optimized on mini-batches via Adam on combined Loss $\mathcal{L} = (z - v)^2 - \boldsymbol{\pi}^T \log \boldsymbol{p} + c\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$.
Step 6
Eval & Elo Benchmark
Model benchmarked in $E$ evaluation games vs random bot to compute win rate $W$ and updated Elo rating.
1. Spatial Input State Representation
Tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times N \times N}$
At turn $t$, the game board state $s_t$ is encoded into a 10-plane binary feature tensor $X(s)$:
$$X(s) = \left[ X_{\text{player}}, X_{\text{opp}}, X_{\text{lib}_1}^P, X_{\text{lib}_2}^P, X_{\text{lib}_{\ge3}}^P, X_{\text{lib}_1}^O, X_{\text{lib}_2}^O, X_{\text{lib}_{\ge3}}^O, X_{\text{ko}}, X_{\text{turn}} \right]$$
  • Planes 0–1 ($X_{\text{player}}, X_{\text{opp}}$): Binary stone positions for current player and opponent ($1$ if stone present, $0$ otherwise).
  • Planes 2–4 ($X_{\text{lib}_k}^P$): Liberty count buckets for current player's stone chains (chains with 1, 2, or $\ge 3$ liberties).
  • Planes 5–7 ($X_{\text{lib}_k}^O$): Liberty count buckets for opponent stone chains.
  • Plane 8 ($X_{\text{ko}}$): Illegal Ko move mask (prevents infinite legal move loops).
  • Plane 9 ($X_{\text{turn}}$): Player color plane ($1$ for Black, $0$ for White).
2. Dual-Head Residual Network Architecture
ResNet Body + Policy & Value Heads
The feature tensor $X$ passes through a Conv2D stem, $K$ residual blocks with skip connections, and splits into policy and value heads:
$$\text{Stem: } H_0 = \text{ReLU}\big(\text{BatchNorm}(\text{Conv}_{3\times3}(X))\big)$$ $$\text{ResBlock } l: \mathbf{y}_l = \text{ReLU}\big(\text{BN}(\text{Conv}_{3\times3}(H_{l-1}))\big), \quad H_l = \text{ReLU}\big(H_{l-1} + \text{BN}(\text{Conv}_{3\times3}(\mathbf{y}_l))\big)$$
$$\text{Policy Head: } \mathbf{p}(a|s) = \text{Softmax}\Big( W_p \cdot \text{flatten}\big(\text{ReLU}(\text{BN}(\text{Conv}_{1\times1}(H_K)))\big) + \mathbf{b}_p \Big) \in \mathbb{R}^{N^2+1}$$ $$\text{Value Head: } v(s) = \tanh\Big( W_{v2} \cdot \text{ReLU}\big( W_{v1} \cdot \text{flatten}(\text{ReLU}(\text{BN}(\text{Conv}_{1\times1}(H_K)))) + \mathbf{b}_{v1} \big) + b_{v2} \Big) \in [-1, +1]$$
The Policy Head outputs prior probabilities $P(a|s)$ for all $N^2$ board positions plus 1 Pass move. The Value Head outputs scalar outcome prediction $v(s) \in [-1, +1]$ from the mover's perspective.
3. MCTS Search & PUCT Selection
Upper Confidence Bound
During tree search, moves are selected to maximize the Predictor Upper Confidence Bound (PUCT) score:
$$\text{UCB}(s, a) = Q(s, a) + c_{\text{puct}} \cdot P(s, a) \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{b} N(s, b)}}{1 + N(s, a)}$$
  • $Q(s, a) = \frac{W(s, a)}{N(s, a)}$ is the mean action value accumulated from subtree evaluations.
  • $P(s, a)$ is the prior move probability supplied by the policy head.
  • $N(s, a)$ is the visit count for candidate action $a$.
  • First Play Urgency (FPU): Unvisited nodes ($N=0$) estimate $Q(s,a) = -Q_{\text{parent}} - f_{\text{reduction}}$ to prevent premature uniform widening.
$$\text{Dirichlet Root Noise: } P'(s_{\text{root}}, a) = (1 - \epsilon) P(s_{\text{root}}, a) + \epsilon \cdot \eta_a, \quad \boldsymbol{\eta} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha=0.3)$$
Injected at the root during self-play ($\epsilon = 0.25$) to guarantee opening diversity and prevent premature convergence. During backup, values negate at alternating plies: $v_{\text{parent}} \leftarrow -v_{\text{child}}$.
3b. Deep Dive: What is an MCTS Simulation & How Does the Decision Tree Work?
Alternating Minimax Tree Lookahead
Does MCTS play Black's moves for each move and make a decision tree on where White might go?
YES, EXACTLY! Monte Carlo Tree Search builds an alternating 2-player decision tree to evaluate candidate moves before making a final decision.

When it is Black's turn at root board state $s_0$, MCTS does not make a blind guess. Instead, it runs $M$ lookahead simulations (e.g. 50–100 simulations). Each single simulation performs 4 distinct steps:

  1. 1. Selection (Simulating Alternating Black & White Turns down the Tree):
    Starting at $s_0$ (Black's turn), MCTS picks Black's candidate move $a_1$ with the highest PUCT score ($Q + U$). This leads to state $s_1$, where it is White's turn. At $s_1$, MCTS simulates where White might respond by evaluating White's candidate moves $a_2$ using PUCT from White's perspective! This forms an alternating decision tree: $$\text{Root } s_0 (\text{Black}) \xrightarrow{a_1} s_1 (\text{White}) \xrightarrow{a_2} s_2 (\text{Black}) \xrightarrow{a_3} s_3 (\text{White}) \dots s_L$$
  2. 2. Expansion & Neural Network Forward Pass:
    When the search reaches an unexplored leaf position $s_L$, MCTS expands the node and queries the dual-head Residual Neural Network: $$f_\theta(s_L) = (\boldsymbol{p}(a|s_L), \; v(s_L))$$ The Policy Head supplies prior probabilities $\boldsymbol{p}$ for legal moves at $s_L$. The Value Head returns scalar evaluation $v(s_L) \in [-1, +1]$ predicting the expected outcome from the perspective of the player whose turn it is at $s_L$.
  3. 3. Backpropagation (Zero-Sum Value Negation):
    The outcome prediction $v(s_L)$ travels back up the path to the root $s_0$. Because Go is a zero-sum game, Black's gain is White's loss: $$v_{\text{parent}} = -v_{\text{child}}$$ At each node along the path, visit count $N(s,a)$ is incremented by $+1$, and total action value $W(s,a)$ is updated ($W \leftarrow W + v$).
  4. 4. Final Move Selection:
    After repeating this 4-step simulation $M$ times, root node $s_0$ has accumulated visit counts $N(s_0, a)$ across all candidate branches. The move $a^*$ with the most visits is chosen as Black's actual played move!

💡 Search Depth & Why MCTS Doesn't Explode Exponentially ($10 \times 10 = 100$):
  • How Deep Does a Simulation Go? Depth is asymmetric and variable. Each simulation travels down the tree until it hits an unvisited state $s_L$. Unpromising lines are evaluated in 1–2 moves, while critical tactical fights (like a capturing race) are revisited across multiple simulations, deepening 10 to 30+ moves deep down that single sharp line!
  • How Simulation Budget Limits Search: A simulation budget of $M = 50$ means MCTS executes exactly 50 single lookahead paths from $s_0$. It does not explore all $80^{10} \approx 10^{19}$ full combinations.
  • Neural Network Policy Pruning: Out of 80 legal moves, the NN policy head immediately assigns low probabilities ($P \approx 0.001$) to weak moves. PUCT ignores those bad moves, concentrating ~90% of the 50 simulations into the top 2–3 strongest candidate lines.
3c. Territory Estimation & Chinese Area Scoring Algorithm
Area Scoring: $S = \text{Stones} + \text{Territory}$
Territory estimation in Chinese Area Scoring evaluates position equity without requiring manual dead-stone removal agreements. The system computes territory and score using a 4-step spatial algorithm:
$$\text{Black Score} = S_B + T_B, \qquad \text{White Score} = S_W + T_W + \text{Komi}$$ $$\text{Estimated Lead} = \text{Black Score} - \text{White Score}$$
The 4-Step Flood-Fill Territory Algorithm:
  1. Step 1 (Stone Counting): Count all living Black stones ($S_B = \sum \mathbb{I}[grid = 1]$) and White stones ($S_W = \sum \mathbb{I}[grid = 2]$) on the $N \times N$ grid.
  2. Step 2 (Empty Region Discovery): Perform Breadth-First Search (BFS) on all empty intersections ($grid[r][c] = 0$) to group connected empty spaces into discrete regions $R_1, R_2, \dots, R_k$.
  3. Step 3 (Border Color Inspection): For each empty region $R_k$, inspect the colors of all adjacent stones bordering the perimeter:
    • If the perimeter consists exclusively of Black stones $\rightarrow R_k$ is Black Territory ($T_B += |R_k|$).
    • If the perimeter consists exclusively of White stones $\rightarrow R_k$ is White Territory ($T_W += |R_k|$).
    • If the perimeter touches both Black and White stones $\rightarrow R_k$ is Neutral / Dame ($T = 0$).
  4. Step 4 (Area Scoring Summary): Add total territory $T_B, T_W$ to stone counts $S_B, S_W$ and add Komi to White.
4. Temperature Scaling & Symmetry Augmentation
Replay Buffer (50,000 states)
After $M$ simulations, the search visit distribution forms policy target vector $\boldsymbol{\pi}$:
$$\pi_a = \frac{N(s, a)^{1/\tau}}{\sum_{b} N(s, b)^{1/\tau}}$$
  • Exploratory phase ($\tau = 1.0$): Plays early moves (first 15–30 turns) probabilistically according to $\boldsymbol{\pi}$.
  • Greedy phase ($\tau \to 0$): Selects the move with maximum visit count $\text{argmax}_a N(s, a)$.
8-Fold Dihedral Symmetry ($D_4$): When sampling mini-batches from the Replay Buffer, positions are randomly augmented using 4 rotations $\times$ 2 reflections ($k \in \{0 \dots 7\}$), expanding data efficiency 8-fold without extra self-play compute.
5. Neural Network Loss Function & Optimization
Adam + Cosine LR Schedule
The neural network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are optimized to minimize the combined loss $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ over sampled mini-batches:
$$\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \underbrace{(z - v_\theta(s))^2}_{\text{Value MSE Loss } \mathcal{L}_v} \;+\; \underbrace{\left( -\sum_{a=1}^{N^2+1} \pi_a \log p_\theta(a|s) \right)}_{\text{Policy Cross-Entropy Loss } \mathcal{L}_p} \;+\; c \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$$
  • $z \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$ is the actual game outcome (scored by Chinese Area Rules).
  • $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is the target MCTS visit probability distribution vector.
  • $c \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$ is $L_2$ weight decay ($c = 10^{-4}$) preventing parameter explosion.
  • Gradients are clipped at maximum norm $\|\mathbf{g}\|_2 \le 1.0$ before the Adam step.
6. Elo Rating & Skill Progression
Anchor Baseline = 500 Elo
Model strength is measured by playing $E$ eval games against a uniform random bot. The new Elo rating $R_{\text{new}}$ updates according to actual win rate $W$:
$$E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_{\text{anchor}} - R_{\text{model}})/400}}, \quad R_{\text{new}} = R_{\text{old}} + K \cdot (W_{\text{actual}} - E_A)$$

🛡 Champion vs Candidate — the Promotion Gate

Training keeps two copies of the network. Only one of them is allowed to generate self-play games. This is the single most important safety mechanism in the loop: the network that plays the training games decides what every future iteration learns from, so letting a worse network take that job poisons everything downstream.

The two networks

Gate: 55% over 10 games
Role In code What it does
Champion trainer.eval_network The reigning best network. Generates every self-play game, plays the eval matches, and is the opponent you face on the Play page. Changes only when a candidate beats it.
Candidate trainer.network The network being actively trained by gradient descent each iteration. It never touches self-play until it earns promotion.

Each iteration the candidate is trained on the replay buffer, then plays 10 games against the champion with colours alternating. If it scores ≥ 55% it is promoted and becomes the new champion. If not, the candidate is discarded as a generator and the champion keeps its job.

Colours alternate deliberately — a network that only wins as Black scores ~50% and cannot pass.

What gate_promoted means for learning

gate_promoted = true
The candidate genuinely plays better. It becomes the champion, so the next batch of self-play games is generated at a higher level. Stronger games → better training targets → the model advances. This is normal, healthy progress.
gate_promoted = false
The update made the network worse. It is refused, and the champion generates the next games. Training does not stop — the candidate keeps training on fresh champion-quality data and can try again next iteration. A rejection is the system working, not an error.
Stall breaker:
After 5 consecutive rejections the candidate is assumed to have drifted somewhere it cannot recover from, and is reset back to the champion's weights so it retrains from a known-good starting point instead of deadlocking forever.

Expect a healthy run to promote most — but not all — iterations. A long streak of rejections means learning has stalled: lower the learning rate, or add more self-play games so the buffer carries more signal.

Where the champion is stored

Both networks live in the model's single checkpoint, models/<model-id>/weights.pt, under separate keys:

KeyContents
champion_state_dictThe gated champion — restored as the self-play generator on restart.
model_state_dictThe training candidate, plus optimizer_state_dict so training resumes mid-stride.
Checkpoints saved before gating existed have no champion_state_dict; those fall back to using the training weights as the champion, so old models still load.

🚨 The Collapse Guard

A monitor, not a blocker. It runs once per iteration and reports two numbers that reveal a specific failure the ordinary loss metrics cannot see.

📖 What it is actually detecting
Detection & Warning
The failure: value-head collapse
When one colour starts winning nearly every game, the value head can drive its loss to almost zero by ignoring the board entirely and reading only the turn-colour input plane — outputting +1 whenever Black is to move and −1 whenever White is. Value loss looks excellent (≈0.03) while the network has stopped evaluating positions altogether.
Why that is fatal
MCTS picks moves by comparing the values of candidate positions. If every position returns the same number, every move looks identical, and the search degenerates into following the raw policy. Your simulations per move stop doing any work at all.

The two tripwires

MetricHealthyTrips atMeaning
value_std_black
value_std_white		 > 0.05 < 0.05 Spread of the value head within each colour. Near zero means it is predicting from turn colour alone.
pass_rate_black
pass_rate_white		 < 15% > 15% Share of a colour's moves that are passes. Under area scoring a pass donates points, so heavy passing bleeds the game away.

The spread must be measured separately for each colour. Across mixed positions a fully collapsed head still looks perfectly healthy — the values are bimodal at ±1, giving a standard deviation near 1.0. Only splitting by side to move exposes that each group is flat.

⚙️ Does it actually stop anything?
Read this
By default — no
The collapse guard is a smoke alarm, not a sprinkler. It emits a collapse_warning event to the training log and writes the four numbers into every metrics row. Training continues. collapse_auto_stop is false by default.
What actually prevents collapse
The promotion gate is the real defence. Because gate matches alternate colours, a colour-degenerate network scores about 50% and never gets promoted — so it can never become the generator and poison the next iteration's data. The guard tells you why the gate started rejecting.
If you want it to halt automatically:
Set collapse_auto_stop: true in the model's config.json. Training then stops the moment either tripwire fires, instead of only logging it.

🚨 Red Flags & Diagnostic Troubleshooting (When to Take Charge)

💥 Loss Explosion / NaN / Infs
Critical Red Flag
Observed Symptoms
Policy Loss or Value Loss suddenly displays NaN, Infinity, or spikes to > 50.0 in the training metrics log.
Root Cause
Learning rate is set too high, causing gradient steps to overshoot local minima and corrupt network weights.
Action Required:
Lower Learning Rate in the command bar tuner (e.g. from 0.002 to 0.0005). If weights are corrupted with NaN, stop training and revert to a previous checkpoint.
📉 Policy Loss Flatlines High (> 3.2 after 15+ Iterations)
Attention Needed
Observed Symptoms
Policy loss stays near the initial uniform entropy baseline ($\approx \ln(N^2+1) \approx 4.4$ for 9×9) and doesn't drop below 3.2.
Root Cause
MCTS Simulations count is too low (< 30). With few simulations, tree visits remain flat, creating noisy, near-uniform policy targets $\boldsymbol{\pi}$.
Action Required:
Increase MCTS Simulations to 100–200. This sharpens the visit distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ so the policy head has clear move targets to learn.
🎲 Win Rate vs Random Stuck at ~50%
Stagnation Warning
Observed Symptoms
After 10+ iterations, the model still loses games to the random bot and win rate hovers between 45% – 60%.
Root Cause
Search lookahead is insufficient to find basic ladder/capture tactics, or network size is undersized for board dimensions (e.g. Tiny net on 19×19).
Action Required:
Raise MCTS Simulations to at least 100. If playing on 13×13 or 19×19, ensure you are using a Medium or Large network.
🌊 Value Loss Lingers High (> 0.50)
Data Unbalance
Observed Symptoms
Value loss oscillates wildly between 0.40 and 0.80 instead of smoothly settling into 0.15–0.30.
Root Cause
Replay buffer is underfilled or self-play games per iteration is too low (e.g. 1–2 games), causing network to overfit to extreme outcomes of a single game.
Action Required:
Increase Self-Play Games per Iteration from 5 to 15–20. This populates the buffer with diverse positions and stabilizes value targets.
☯ Extreme Color Skew (Black or White Wins 100%)
Komi Mismatch
Observed Symptoms
Self-play win balance bar on Training page shows 100% Black wins or 100% White wins across multiple iterations.
Root Cause
Komi is misconfigured (e.g., set to 0.0 or 15.0), introducing structural imbalance that makes one side unplayable.
Action Required:
Verify Komi is set to 6.5 for 9×9 boards or 7.5 for 19×19 boards.
⚔ Premature Passing / Hesitation to Kill 1-Liberty Groups
Temperature Cliff
Observed Symptoms
Bots pass at move 40–60 while enemy groups with 1 liberty (atari) remain uncaptured, or seem hesitant to initiate tactical captures late in the game.
Root Cause
Temperature dropped to near-zero ($0.001$) too early via a hard step function, freezing MCTS move selection into rigid, single-branch choices before tactical fights resolved.
Action Required:
Ensure Temp Decay Moves is set to at least 30 moves, and set Temp Final to 0.1 instead of 0.001 to maintain tactical alertness.
✅ Healthy Training Benchmark Checklist
All Systems Optimal
Your model is learning effectively if you observe all of the following:
  • Win rate vs Random Bot: Exceeds 90% by iteration 8–10 and hits 100% by iteration 15.
  • Policy Loss: Shows a steady downward trajectory from ~3.8 down to 1.2–1.8.
  • Value Loss: Settles into the 0.15–0.30 band without wild spikes.
  • Elo Progression: Climbs smoothly from 500 up past 1000 (15k rank) within 15–20 iterations.
  • Self-play Balance: Black and White win proportions fluctuate near 45%–55% equity.
💡

Golden Rule for Tuning

When you want to boost model performance, the highest-impact interventions — in order — are: 1. Increase MCTS Simulations (sharpens move targets $\boldsymbol{\pi}$), 2. Increase Self-Play Games (stabilizes value $z$), and 3. Increase Network Capacity. Keep c_puct (1.5) and Learning Rate (0.002) at their defaults unless diagnosing a specific anomaly above.
